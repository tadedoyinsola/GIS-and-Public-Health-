Kentucky Lung Cancer Mortality, 2000–2004

An interactive geospatial analysis by Tolulope Adedoyin Oladeji  |  View source on GitHub

This interactive map explores county-level lung cancer mortality patterns across Kentucky, examining how mortality rates and total death counts differ between Black and White males during 2000–2004. The analysis identifies the two counties — Knox and Gallatin — where both racial groups exceeded a mortality threshold of 150 per 100,000, representing cross-population priority areas for public health intervention.

Background

Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer death in the United States, and Kentucky consistently records among the highest age-adjusted mortality rates in the nation. The geographic distribution of this burden is uneven: certain Appalachian counties experience dramatically elevated rates, driven by a combination of historical smoking prevalence, occupational exposure in coal-mining economies, and disparities in screening and healthcare access.

This analysis uses publicly available data from the National Cancer Institute's Cancer Mortality Maps to map and interrogate that geographic variation at the county level. The interactive map allows viewers to toggle between rate-based and count-based perspectives, examine the methodological limitations of race-specific county-level estimates, and identify the cross-population priority counties.

Key Findings

Two cross-population priority counties identified. Knox County in the southeastern Appalachian region and Gallatin County in the north both exceeded the 150-per-100,000 mortality threshold for Black and White males simultaneously. Knox represents a high-confidence epidemiological signal consistent with the broader Appalachian pattern; Gallatin, given its small population, warrants careful interpretation for small-numerator effects.

Pattern 1: Spatially coherent White male mortality in eastern Kentucky

Fourteen Kentucky counties exhibit White male mortality rates exceeding 150 per 100,000 — a tight cluster across the eastern Appalachian region including Lee, Owsley, Knott, McCreary, Perry, and Knox counties. In line with documented epidemiological patterns, this concentration reflects shared regional drivers spanning occupational exposure, smoking prevalence, and healthcare access disparities.

Pattern 2: Statistical fragility of race-specific county-level rates

Of Kentucky's 120 counties, 55 (45.8%) recorded a Black male mortality rate of zero in the source data — reflecting absent or negligible Black populations rather than true zero mortality. According to standard public health surveillance practice, these values are best treated as missing rather than as substantive zeros. This methodological consideration is essential for responsible interpretation of race-stratified county-level data in low-diversity states.

Pattern 3: Rates and counts identify different things

The five counties with the highest total male lung cancer death counts — Jefferson (Louisville), Fayette (Lexington), Kenton, Daviess, and Warren — cluster around urban population centers, while the highest-rate counties cluster in rural Appalachia. This contrast illustrates a fundamental public health surveillance principle: rates identify where risk is elevated, while counts identify where total burden is concentrated. Effective intervention planning requires both perspectives — targeted risk reduction in rural high-rate counties, alongside absolute case-volume management in urban high-count counties.

Methodology

The interactive map is generated entirely in Python using the open-source geospatial stack. County boundaries and mortality data come from the National Cancer Institute Cancer Mortality Maps tool. The Python implementation performs spatial filtering, projection management, attribute-based selection, and choropleth construction; the resulting interactive map uses Folium (a Python wrapper for Leaflet.js) to produce a self-contained HTML artifact.

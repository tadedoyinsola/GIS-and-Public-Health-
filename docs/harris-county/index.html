Harris County, Texas — Tract-Level Geography of Health and Housing

An interactive geospatial analysis by Tolulope Adedoyin Oladeji  |  View source on GitHub

This interactive map explores four tract-level indicators across Houston's home county — population density, vacant housing units, poverty rate, and unemployment rate — across 649 census tracts and 56 cities. The analysis was originally designed for emergency-response housing-allocation planning in the aftermath of Hurricane Katrina, when displaced persons from New Orleans permanently relocated to the Houston metropolitan area.

Background

Harris County is the third most populous county in the United States and the largest in Texas. Its tract-level geography reveals dramatic internal variation — dense urban cores within Houston proper, suburban industrial zones in Pasadena and Baytown along the Houston Ship Channel, and unincorporated rural land at the county's outer edges. Understanding this heterogeneity is essential for any spatially-targeted public health intervention, housing policy, or emergency-response planning effort.

In 2005, Hurricane Katrina displaced an estimated 250,000 residents from New Orleans, many of whom permanently relocated to the Houston area. The vacant housing landscape of Harris County in 2000 — captured in this dataset — represents the absorption capacity that the region brought to that crisis. Understanding which tracts had elevated vacancy, and how those tracts correlated with poverty and unemployment, illuminates both the immediate response geography and the longer-term integration outcomes.

Key Findings

Two cities, two morphologies. Pasadena exhibits a dense urban-core pattern with high-density tracts clustered in the western half of the city, while Baytown shows a concentrated-node pattern — high density restricted to the city core itself, surrounded by very low-density unincorporated land. For housing-absorption planning, these two morphologies imply fundamentally different intervention strategies.

Pattern 1: Density and vacancy co-localize, but not perfectly

Census tracts with the highest population density generally show the largest number of vacant housing units in absolute terms — an expected pattern, since more housing units means more potential vacancies. However, certain Pasadena tracts show disproportionately large vacant-housing markers relative to their density class, suggesting building-level vacancy concentrations (perhaps aging multi-family complexes) worth investigating for targeted housing-quality interventions.

Pattern 2: Poverty and unemployment spatially overlap

Across the 10-city Map Series, high-poverty tracts and high-unemployment tracts overlap substantially, consistent with the documented structural relationship between labor-market exclusion and household poverty. However, certain tracts show divergence — elevated poverty with only moderate unemployment, suggesting reliance on informal-sector or low-wage employment rather than complete labor-market exclusion. These divergence tracts are priority targets for workforce-development programs rather than direct income-support interventions.

Pattern 3: Dual-encoding cartography illuminates joint vulnerability

The combined choropleth-and-graduated-points symbology used throughout this analysis allows simultaneous visualization of two related but distinct dimensions of population vulnerability — a denominator effect (density, choropleth) and a magnitude effect (counts or rates, point size). Spatial co-occurrence becomes immediately legible in ways that two separate single-variable maps would obscure. According to standard public health surveillance cartography, this dual-encoding approach is recommended for any analysis where two indicators jointly define a priority zone for intervention.

Methodology

All four indicators are visualized using quantile classification (5 classes), which divides each variable's distribution into five groups containing approximately equal numbers of tracts (~130 each). This classification scheme supports fair visual comparison across layers — a "darkest-class" tract on the poverty map is in the top 20% for poverty, and a "darkest-class" tract on the unemployment map is in the top 20% for unemployment — even though the absolute breakpoint values differ.

Spatial data is reprojected to NAD83 / Texas South Central State Plane (EPSG:32140) for the static cartographic analysis and to WGS84 lat/lon (EPSG:4326) for the web map. The interactive map uses Folium (a Python wrapper for Leaflet.js) to produce a self-contained HTML artifact, with custom zoom-to-city buttons providing one-click navigation to Pasadena and Baytown as focal cities of comparison.
